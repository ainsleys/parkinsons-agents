# Parkinson's Disease: Genetics, Epidemiology, and Environmental/Lifestyle Risk Factors (literature through 2026)

Scope note: These notes cover (a) monogenic and risk-gene genetics of PD, including GWAS, heritability, polygenic risk, and ancestry diversity; and (b) epidemiology, environmental and lifestyle risk/protective factors, causal-inference (Mendelian randomization) evidence, prodromal markers, and the major cohorts/consortia. Effect sizes are reported as given in the cited source. Where a figure comes from a secondary review rather than the primary paper, the review is cited. Research date: October 2026.

---

## Key Question 1: Monogenic causes and major risk genes — frequencies, penetrance, phenotypes

### Takeaway
Monogenic PD (autosomal dominant SNCA, LRRK2, VPS35; autosomal recessive PRKN, PINK1, PARK7/DJ-1) explains only a few percent of cases overall but a large share of juvenile/early-onset disease; GBA1 variants are the most common genetic risk factor (up to ~5% of PD; 15–20% in Ashkenazi Jews), LRRK2 G2019S is the most common dominant cause with incomplete, ancestry-dependent penetrance (~25–42% by age 80), and RAB32 Ser71Arg (2024) is the newest confirmed dominant risk gene, acting via LRRK2 kinase activation.

### Cited Findings

**Overview / inheritance patterns**
- Monogenic PD includes autosomal recessive forms (PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1/PARK7) and autosomal dominant forms (LRRK2, SNCA, VPS35) — [Monogenic Parkinson's Disease: Genotype, Phenotype, Pathophysiology, and Genetic Testing, Genes 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471); see also [GeneReviews: Monogenic Parkinson Disease Overview, NCBI Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK1223/).
- Heritability of PD from twin and family studies is estimated at "at least 27% and up to 60%"; more recent estimates place it between 22% and 40% in Europeans — [The Genetics of Parkinson's Disease and Implications for Clinical Practice, Genes 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4425/12/7/1006).
- Longitudinal analysis of the Swedish Twin Registry found concordance of 11% in monozygotic and 4% in same-sex dizygotic pairs, heritability ~34% — [Wirdefeldt et al., Heritability of Parkinson disease in Swedish twins: a longitudinal study, Neurobiology of Aging 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0197458011000467); earlier cross-sectional twin work found little heritability — [Tanner et al., Parkinson Disease in Twins: An Etiologic Study, JAMA 1999](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/188406); [Wirdefeldt et al., No evidence for heritability of Parkinson disease in Swedish twins, Neurology 2004](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.WNL.0000129841.30587.9D).
- Having a first-degree relative with PD is one of the strongest risk factors: OR 3.23 (95% CI 2.65–3.93); any relative OR 4.45 (3.39–5.83); any relative with tremor OR 2.74 (2.10–3.57) — [Noyce et al., Meta-analysis of early nonmotor features and risk factors for Parkinson disease, Annals of Neurology 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687).

**PRKN (Parkin, PARK2) — autosomal recessive**
- PRKN mutations are the most common cause of early-onset PD (EOPD), particularly in Europeans; the proportion of probands with PRKN mutations rises with younger onset: 42.2% for onset ≤20 y, 29% for 21–30 y, 13% for 31–40 y — [Genes 2022 monogenic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471).
- Genetics of recessive forms reviewed in [Genetics of Parkinson's disease: Recessive forms, 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667049624000036).

**PINK1 (PARK6) — autosomal recessive**
- Biallelic PINK1 mutations are the second most common cause of EOPD worldwide (1–5% of EOPD), more frequent in North Africa; median age at onset 32 years; 62% early-onset, 22% late, 15% juvenile — [Genes 2022 monogenic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471).

**PARK7 / DJ-1 — autosomal recessive**
- Prevalence among EOPD patients 0.4–1% — [Genes 2022 monogenic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471).

**SNCA (PARK1/4) — autosomal dominant (point mutations and multiplications)**
- SNCA mutations are rare: 0.045–1.1% frequency in recent studies — [Genes 2022 monogenic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471). Genotype–phenotype relations systematically reviewed in the MDSGene series — [Trinh et al., Genotype-phenotype relations for the Parkinson's disease genes SNCA, LRRK2, VPS35: MDSGene systematic review, Movement Disorders 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328498305_Genotype-phenotype_relations_for_the_Parkinson's_disease_genes_SNCA_LRRK2_VPS35_MDSGene_systematic_review_MDSGene_Systematic_Review_SNCA_LRRK2_VPS35).

**VPS35 (PARK17, D620N) — autosomal dominant**
- Overall prevalence ~0.115% of PD, up to ~1% of autosomal dominant PD — [Genes 2022 monogenic review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes13030471).

**LRRK2 (PARK8) — autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance; G2019S is the most common pathogenic variant**
- Highest G2019S frequencies are in North African Arab-Berber (30–41%) and Ashkenazi Jewish (6–30%) PD populations due to founder effects; in Ashkenazi Jews, 30% of familial PD, 14% of sporadic PD, 2.0% of controls; in North African Berbers 37% familial, 41% sporadic, 1% controls — [Age at onset of LRRK2 p.Gly2019Ser PD across ancestries, medRxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.04.25327685.full.pdf); [Frequency of LRRK2 G2019S in South African PD, Neurogenetics 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10048-019-00588-z).
- G2019S is absent in Nigerian PD cohorts — [LRRK2 G2019S mutation is absent in a second cohort of Nigerian Africans with PD, 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6277104/).
- Penetrance in Ashkenazi Jewish carriers estimated at only ~26% (Michael J. Fox Ashkenazi Jewish LRRK2 Consortium) — [Marder et al., Age-specific penetrance of LRRK2 G2019S in the Michael J. Fox Ashkenazi Jewish LRRK2 Consortium, Neurology 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26062626); in non-Ashkenazi carriers penetrance is 25–42.5% at age 80 — [Lee et al., Penetrance estimate of LRRK2 p.G2019S mutation in individuals of non-Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, Movement Disorders 2017](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.27059).
- Penetrance appears higher in Arab-Berber carriers than in US Ashkenazi Jews and Europeans (ancestral population-specific effects) — [medRxiv 2025 age-at-onset study](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.04.25327685.full.pdf).
- Penetrance of G2019S is modified by polygenic risk score — [Iwaki et al., Penetrance of PD in LRRK2 p.G2019S carriers is modified by a polygenic risk score, bioRxiv 2019 (published Movement Disorders 2020)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/738260v1.full).
- G2019S arose through independent mutational events in humans — [Lesage et al., PD-related LRRK2 G2019S mutation results from independent mutational events in humans, Human Molecular Genetics 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20197411/).

**RAB32 Ser71Arg — new autosomal dominant risk gene (2024)**
- Gustavsson et al. reported RAB32 Ser71Arg as a novel risk factor for autosomal dominant PD with reduced penetrance; mean onset 54.6 y (SD 12.75, range 31–81, n=16), two-thirds with family history; functional assays show RAB32 S71R increases LRRK2 kinase activity (LRRK2 S1292 autophosphorylation), linking it mechanistically to LRRK2 — [Gustavsson et al., RAB32 Ser71Arg in autosomal dominant Parkinson's disease: linkage, association, and functional analyses, Lancet Neurology 2024;23:603–614](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1474442224003247); [University of Florida summary](https://neurology.ufl.edu/2024/06/02/rab32-ser71arg-in-autosomal-dominant-parkinsons-disease-linkage-association-and-functional-analyses); [Lancet Neurology editorial, 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(24)00166-2/abstract).
- Independently identified by systematic rare-variant analysis — [Hop et al., Systematic rare variant analyses identify RAB32 as a susceptibility gene for familial Parkinson's disease, Nature Genetics 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-024-01787-7).
- Replicated in a French cohort; frequency 0.1% of European PD index cases — [Cogan et al., Confirmation of RAB32 Ser71Arg Involvement in Parkinson's Disease, Movement Disorders 2025](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.30024); [Gustavsson editorial, Movement Disorders 2025](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.30080).
- Further characterization: [Genetic and Epidemiological Insights into RAB32-Linked PD, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39460989/); [RAB32-Linked PD: Deep Phenotyping, MDSGene Literature Review, and Application of SynNeurGe Criteria, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41103171/).

**GBA1 — most common genetic risk factor (not Mendelian; dominant risk allele)**
- GBA1 variants are the most common known genetic risk factor and are present in up to 5% of PD cases, 15–20% in Ashkenazi Jewish PD; reported frequencies vary widely (1.8% to 47%) by ethnicity and variants screened — [Vieira et al., Consensus Guidance for Genetic Counseling in GBA1 Variants: A Focus on Parkinson's Disease, Movement Disorders 2024;39(12):2144–2154](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657020/).
- Variant classes: severe (e.g., L444P/p.Leu483Pro, 84GG, IVS2+1) increase PD risk ~9–10-fold; mild (N370S/p.Asn409Ser) ~4-fold; "risk" variants E326K/p.Glu365Lys and T369M/p.Thr408Met do not cause Gaucher disease but increase PD risk. Meta-analytic ORs: N370S 2.2–7.8; L444P 6.4–30.4 across populations — [Vieira et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657020/).
- Age-specific penetrance for heterozygous carriers: 1.5–4.7% at age 60, 7.7–9.1% at age 80 — [Vieira et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657020/). A 2025 analysis gives higher estimates (~7.6% at 50 to 29.7% at 80 under a dominant model), with penetrance depending on variant severity and polygenic background; GBA1 pathogenic variants present in 8.8% of PD in UK Biobank and 9.9% in LuxPark, and carriers show higher risk across all PRS strata — [Penetrance of PD in GBA1 carriers depends on variant severity and polygenic background, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506446/).
- Phenotype: onset earlier by ~2–10 years, accelerated motor and cognitive decline, increased depression, RBD and hyposmia — [Vieira et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657020/).
- Variant detection and pathogenicity scoring is a methodological issue in GBA1 studies — [GBA1 in Parkinson's disease: variant detection and pathogenicity scoring matters, BMC Genomics 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12864-023-09417-y); global perspective reviewed in [A Global Perspective of GBA1-Related PD, Genes 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4425/15/12/1605).
- African-ancestry-specific GBA1 risk: the intronic variant rs3115534-G, identified in the first GP2-supported African GWAS (1,488 cases, 196,430 controls), is "much more common" in African/African-admixed ancestry; it is the most common damaging GBA1 variant, with minor allele frequency >20% in some populations — [Rizig et al., Identification of genetic risk loci and causal insights associated with PD in African and African admixed populations: a GWAS, Lancet Neurology 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(23)00283-1/abstract); [GP2 summary](https://gp2.org/genetic-representation-in-parkinsons-research-drives-progress/). CRISPR editing showed rs3115534 disrupts an intronic branchpoint, producing intron-8-retaining transcripts, reduced GBA1 expression and glucocerebrosidase activity — [African ancestry neurodegeneration risk variant disrupts an intronic branchpoint in GBA1, Nature Structural & Molecular Biology 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41594-024-01423-2). rs3115534 is also associated with RBD in Nigerians — [GP2](https://gp2.org/genetic-representation-in-parkinsons-research-drives-progress/).

**Common risk loci with functional follow-up: TMEM175, MAPT**
- At the chromosome 4p16.3 locus, knockdown screens found only TMEM175 consistently influenced phosphorylated α-synuclein accumulation; the p.M393T variant shows partial loss of function (reduced lysosomal pH regulation, reduced lysosomal localization, increased p-α-syn) and is proposed as the driver of the GWAS signal — [Jinn et al., Functionalization of the TMEM175 p.M393T variant as a risk factor for Parkinson disease, Human Molecular Genetics 2019](https://academic.oup.com/hmg/article/28/19/3244/5520430); reviewed in [Wang et al., What We Know About TMEM175 in Parkinson's Disease, CNS Neuroscience & Therapeutics 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cns.70195).
- The MAPT H1 haplotype is a leading common risk variant from GWAS; H1 homozygotes show accelerated progression to dementia; different MAPT haplotypes associate with PD vs PSP — [Different MAPT haplotypes are associated with PD and PSP, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19879020); 17q21.31 sub-haplotypes underlying H1 risk associate with LRRC37A/2 expression in astrocytes — [Bowles et al., 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9284779/).

**Monogenic research networks**
- The MJFF Global Monogenic PD Network (within GP2) is building large cohorts of carriers — [Embracing Monogenic Parkinson's Disease: The MJFF Global Genetic PD Cohort, Movement Disorders 2023](https://www.ovid.com/journals/mdis/fulltext/10.1002/mds.29288~embracing-monogenic-parkinsons-disease-the-mjff-global); 59 centers contribute to GP2 monogenic research as of Dec 2025 — [Flores-Ocampo et al., GP2: Advancing genetic discovery and capacity building worldwide, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13435180/).

### Inferences
- The field is converging on a mechanistic axis linking LRRK2 kinase activity, RAB GTPases (RAB32, and by extension RAB29/VPS35 pathways), and lysosomal biology (GBA1, TMEM175), suggesting "genetically distinct causes share the same mechanism" as the Lancet Neurology RAB32 paper itself argued.
- Penetrance figures for LRRK2 and GBA1 are increasingly understood to be modified by polygenic background, which explains the wide ranges in older literature and matters for genetic counseling.
- The GBA1 rs3115534 story shows that ancestry-specific common variants of large effect were missed for two decades because of Eurocentric sampling.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the primary MDSGene systematic review for recessive genes (PRKN/PINK1/DJ-1) with its exact tallies; figures above are from the Genes 2022 review.
- Precise age-specific penetrance tables for Marder 2015 (e.g., penetrance at 60/70/80) could not be fetched (PubMed blocked); only the ~26% headline figure is reported.
- No reliable figure was found for the overall share of all PD attributable to monogenic causes in unselected populations (commonly cited as ~5–10%, but I did not retrieve a primary source).

## Key Question 2: GWAS findings, heritability explained, polygenic risk scores, ancestry diversity (Nalls 2019, Kim 2024, GP2)

### Takeaway
Nalls 2019 (90 loci; common variants explain ~22% of liability, 16–36% of heritability depending on prevalence assumptions) and Kim 2024 (78 loci including 12 novel, first multi-ancestry meta-analysis) define the common-variant architecture; GP2 (launched 2020, >100,000 samples analyzed, 343,000 expected) has added >50 novel risk factors and ancestry-specific discoveries, while PRS performance remains ancestry-dependent and "one-size-fits-all" scores underperform outside Europeans.

### Cited Findings

**Nalls 2019 (IPDGC/23andMe/UK Biobank)**
- Meta-analysis of 17 GWAS: 37,688 cases, 18,618 UK Biobank proxy-cases (first-degree relative with PD), 1.4 million controls; 90 independent risk signals, 38 novel; common variants account for ~22% of PD liability; the 90 loci explain 16–36% of heritable risk depending on prevalence assumptions — [Nalls et al., Identification of novel risk loci, causal insights, and heritable risk for Parkinson's disease: a meta-analysis of GWAS, Lancet Neurology 2019;18(12):1091–1102](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1474442219303205); [Knowledge Portal summary](https://www.kp4cd.org/node/675); PRS deposited in [PGS Catalog PGP000235](https://www.pgscatalog.org/publication/PGP000235/).
- GWAS loci browser: [Grenn et al., The PD GWAS Locus Browser, Movement Disorders 2020](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.28197).
- Known genome-wide significant loci explain ~16% of PD heritability; GWAS overall accounts for 16–36% — [What have we learned from GWAS in Parkinson's disease?, Ageing Research Reviews 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568163722000903).

**Kim 2024 multi-ancestry GWAS (GP2/IPDGC)**
- 49,049 PD cases, 18,785 proxy cases, ~2.4 million controls of European, East Asian, Latin American and African ancestry; 78 independent genome-wide significant loci, 12 potentially novel (MTF2, PIK3CA, ADD1, SYBU, IRS2, USP8, PIGL, FASN, MYLK2, USP25, EP300, PPP6R2); fine-mapped 6 putative causal variants at 6 known loci; 25 potential risk genes nominated — [Kim JJ et al., Multi-ancestry genome-wide association meta-analysis of Parkinson's disease, Nature Genetics 2024;56(1):27–36](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38155330/); [NIA news release](https://www.nia.nih.gov/news/multi-ancestry-analysis-reveals-new-genetic-risk-factors-parkinsons-disease).
- Perspective on global genetics: [Khani et al., Towards a Global View of Parkinson's Disease Genetics, Annals of Neurology 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ana.26905).

**African-ancestry GWAS**
- First African/African-admixed GWAS identified the novel GBA1 rs3115534 locus (see Q1) — [Rizig et al., Lancet Neurology 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(23)00283-1/abstract). An expanded African GWAS identifying ancestry-specific and cross-population loci was posted in 2026 — [Insights Into PD Genetics in African Populations: Expanded GWAS, medRxiv 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.01.26347367v1.full).

**GP2 — Global Parkinson's Genetics Program**
- Launched January 2020 as a five-year initiative funded by Aligning Science Across Parkinson's (ASAP) and implemented by MJFF; emphasis on underrepresented African, Asian, Middle Eastern and Latin American populations. As of December 2025: 436 cohorts across 84 locations; 103,786 samples analyzed; 343,123 expected at completion (more than double the original 150,000 goal); 59 monogenic centers; ~410 trainees (225 from underrepresented populations). GP2-supported studies have identified "more than 50 novel genetic risk factors"; the largest European GWAS cited by GP2 reports 134 independent loci including 59 novel; genotyping uses the NeuroBooster Array with analysis on Terra/Verily cloud platforms — [Flores-Ocampo, Zirra, Tay, van Midden, Mata, Noyce, Periñan et al., The Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2): Advancing genetic discovery and capacity building worldwide, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13435180/); [GP2 program overview](https://gp2.org/genetic-representation-in-parkinsons-research-drives-progress/); earlier framing in [Defining the causes of sporadic PD in GP2, npj Parkinson's Disease 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-023-00533-w).

**Polygenic risk scores and ancestry**
- A 2025 comparative study built 105 PRS across seven ancestries (African admixed, African, Ashkenazi Jewish, Latino/Admixed American, Central Asian, East Asian, European), comparing a 90-variant European-weighted model vs a multi-ancestry p-value-thresholding model; found variable predictive performance, underscoring the limits of a one-size-fits-all approach and the need for larger, ancestry-specific samples — [Insights into ancestral diversity in Parkinson's disease risk: a comparative assessment of polygenic risk scores, npj Parkinson's Disease 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-025-00967-4).
- In South African cohorts, PRS captured a modest but significant proportion of PD susceptibility variance — [Polygenic risk scores and PD in South Africa: Moving towards ancestry-informed disease prediction, PLoS Genetics 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1012064).
- PRS modifies penetrance in LRRK2 G2019S carriers — [Iwaki et al., bioRxiv 2019](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/738260v1.full) — and in GBA1 carriers — [2025 penetrance study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506446/).

### Inferences
- With ~22% of liability attributable to common variants and total heritability estimates of ~22–40%, a substantial but not overwhelming "missing heritability" remains; rare variants (RAB32, GBA1 rare alleles) and ancestry-specific alleles are now the main discovery frontier.
- GP2's expected 343,000 samples will roughly triple the Kim 2024 case count, so loci counts (already ~134 in European-only analyses per GP2) are expected to continue rising through 2026–2027.
- Clinical PRS use remains premature except as a penetrance modifier in carriers of LRRK2/GBA1 variants.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the primary publication/preprint for the "134 loci, 59 novel" European GWAS cited by the GP2 2026 paper (Europe PMC and medRxiv searches failed); the report writer should cite it via the GP2 paper or verify the primary reference.
- Exact SNP-heritability figures from Kim 2024 were not extracted.

## Key Question 3: Epidemiology — global prevalence/incidence, projections, age/sex/geography, and whether incidence is rising independent of aging

### Takeaway
GBD 2021 estimates ~11.8 million people with PD worldwide in 2021 (a 274% rise since 1990); a 2025 BMJ model projects 25.2 million by 2050 (+112%), driven 89% by aging, with East Asia carrying the largest burden and the male:female ratio widening from 1.46 to 1.64. Whether age-standardized incidence is truly rising remains contested: Rochester data showed rising incidence in older men, but Dutch nationwide incidence (2017–2022) was stable and the 2024 Lancet seminar calls rising incidence "less certain."

### Cited Findings

**GBD 2021**
- 11.77 million prevalent cases in 2021 (95% UI 10.44–13.42 million); 6.44 million males, 5.33 million females; ~1.3 million incident cases (0.76 M male, 0.52 M female); 7.47 million DALYs; age-standardized incidence 15.63/100,000 (males 19.72, females 9.12); age-standardized prevalence 138.63/100,000 (males 168.24, females 114.47); 1990–2021 change: incident cases +220%, prevalent cases +274%, DALYs +162%; highest burden East Asia, South Asia, Western Europe; China has the highest age-standardized prevalence (245.73/100,000) and incidence (24.34/100,000); strong positive correlation of rates with sociodemographic index (likely partly reflecting diagnostic capacity); projection to 2035 of ~1.5-fold incidence increase — [Luo et al., Global, regional, national epidemiology and trends of PD 1990–2021: findings from GBD 2021, Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11757241/).
- The GBD 2021 nervous-system paper reports PD prevalence of 11,800 thousand (UI 10,400–13,400), a 273.9% increase 1990–2021 — [GBD 2021 Nervous System Disorders Collaborators, Global, regional, and national burden of disorders affecting the nervous system 1990–2021, Lancet Neurology 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(24)00038-3/fulltext). Additional GBD-2021-based analyses: [Global trends and projections of PD incidence: a 30-year analysis using GBD 2021 data, Journal of Neurology 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13030-2); [Burden of PD in 204 countries 1990–2021, BMC Public Health 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-025-23492-8).
- Earlier benchmark: [GBD 2016 Parkinson's Disease Collaborators, Global, regional, and national burden of PD 1990–2016, Lancet Neurology 2018](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(18)30295-3/fulltext).

**BMJ 2025 projection to 2050**
- 25.2 million people with PD by 2050 (112% increase from 2021); 15.6 M in 2030, 20.4 M in 2040; all-age prevalence 267/100,000 in 2050 (+76%); age-standardized prevalence 216/100,000 (+55%); population aging contributes 89% and population growth 20% of the increase; East Asia 10.9 M cases, South Asia 6.8 M; fastest growth in Western sub-Saharan Africa (+292%), slowest in Central/Eastern Europe (+28%); 2050 prevalence 243/100,000 women vs 295/100,000 men; male:female ratio widening from 1.46 (2021) to 1.64 (2050); ≥80 y prevalence 2,087/100,000; limitations: low data availability/quality in some regions, no ethnic-group resolution, COVID-19 impact not assessed — [Su et al., Projections for prevalence of Parkinson's disease and its driving factors in 195 countries and territories to 2050: modelling study of GBD 2021, BMJ 2025 — BMJ Group press release](https://bmjgroup.com/cases-of-parkinsons-disease-set-to-reach-25-million-worldwide-by-2050/); [MJFF summary](https://www.michaeljfox.org/news/new-estimate-suggests-25-million-people-parkinsons-2050).

**"Parkinson pandemic" framing (Dorsey)**
- PD was described as the fastest-growing neurological disorder; cases doubled 1990–2015 to >6 million, projected to double again to >12 million by 2040 driven by aging, with longevity, declining smoking and industrialization potentially raising the figure to >17 million — [Dorsey & Bloem, The Parkinson Pandemic—A Call to Action, JAMA Neurology 2018](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Parkinson-Pandemic-A-Call-to-Action.-Dorsey-Bloem/240eb91cd395c75525cd7fb21afe5ffb2b94057f); [Dorsey, Sherer, Okun, Bloem, The Emerging Evidence of the Parkinson Pandemic, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329764406_The_Emerging_Evidence_of_the_Parkinson_Pandemic).
- Dorsey and Bloem argue PD is "the world's fastest growing brain disorder," that its rise is attributable primarily to environmental toxicants (pesticides, TCE, air pollution — widespread, mitochondria-damaging, entering by inhalation/ingestion) rather than aging, diagnostics, or genetics alone, and that most cases are preventable — [Dorsey & Bloem, Parkinson's Disease Is Predominantly an Environmental Disease, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2024, DOI 10.3233/JPD-230357](https://doi.org/10.3233/JPD-230357). Policy follow-up: [Dorsey, Okun, Bloem, A PLAN to address the Parkinson pandemic, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1877718X251378115).

**Lancet 2024 seminar**
- PD "is more common in older people and men"; prevalence "has increased over and above demographic changes" internationally, but whether incidence is rising "is less certain," particularly in women and in low/middle-income countries; pesticides implicated as risk; physical activity and smoking tendency protective; ethnic differences may reflect healthcare access; causal inference complicated by the long prodromal period; multipronged prevention advocated — [Ben-Shlomo Y, Darweesh S, Llibre-Guerra J, Marras C, San Luciano M, Tanner C, The epidemiology of Parkinson's disease, Lancet 2024](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(23)01419-8/abstract).

**North America (Parkinson's Foundation Prevalence and Incidence Projects)**
- Prevalence: 930,000 people in the US in 2020, projected 1.2 million by 2030, pooling data from California, Minnesota, Hawaii and Ontario; men more likely to have PD; prevalence increases with age in both sexes — [Marras et al., Prevalence of Parkinson's disease across North America, npj Parkinson's Disease 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326232975_Prevalence_of_Parkinson's_disease_across_North_America); [Parkinson's Foundation Prevalence Project](https://www.parkinson.org/blog/research/prevalence-project).
- Incidence: nearly 90,000 US diagnoses per year (vs prior estimate of 60,000, a ~50% upward revision), based on five epidemiologic datasets for 2012; age-sex-adjusted incidence 108–212/100,000 at ≥65 y and 47–77/100,000 at ≥45 y; incidence increases with age and is higher in males — [Willis et al., Incidence of Parkinson disease in North America, npj Parkinson's Disease 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-022-00410-y); [Parkinson's Foundation statistics](https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/statistics).

**Is incidence rising independent of aging?**
- Rochester Epidemiology Project (Olmsted County, MN, 1976–2005): incidence of parkinsonism and PD increased over 30 years, particularly in men ≥70 y, with elevated risk in the 1915–1924 birth cohort — [Savica et al., Time Trends in the Incidence of Parkinson Disease, JAMA Neurology 2016](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2529538); future-burden implications in [Rocca, The future burden of Parkinson's disease, Movement Disorders 2018](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mds.27114).
- Netherlands nationwide (2017–2022): overall standardized incidence "relatively stable" at 21.8 per 100,000 person-years — [Incidence and spatial variation of Parkinson's disease in the Netherlands (2017–2022), Lancet Regional Health Europe 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanepe/article/PIIS2666-7762(25)00357-6/fulltext).
- GBD-based analyses report that age-standardized YLD rates have not fluctuated much over three decades even as absolute numbers rise — [Journal of Neurology 2025 GBD 2021 analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-13030-2).
- Benchmark population incidence from the Rotterdam Study — [de Lau et al., Incidence of parkinsonism and Parkinson disease in a general population: The Rotterdam Study, Neurology 2004](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.WNL.0000140706.52798.BE).
- Lower-to-upper-middle-income countries: systematic review/meta-analysis of PD prevalence — [npj Parkinson's Disease 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-024-00779-y).

### Inferences
- Sources agree on the direction and approximate magnitude of absolute growth (roughly doubling by 2050) and that aging is the dominant driver; the disagreement is about residual growth after age-standardization (BMJ 2025's +55% age-standardized prevalence increase vs stable Dutch incidence), which is where the "environmental disease" argument lives.
- The widening male:female ratio and the strong SDI correlation both hint at ascertainment and exposure effects layered on biology; the GBD authors themselves flag diagnostic capacity.
- US incidence (~90,000/yr) and prevalence (~1 M) figures are now the standard citations and replaced older ~60,000/yr estimates.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the Darweesh et al. Rotterdam time-trend paper (which reported declining incidence in the Netherlands over earlier decades) to give exact numbers; the report writer should treat "declining incidence in Rotterdam" as plausible but unverified here.
- The BMJ 2025 paper's authors/affiliations were not captured from the press release (first author is Su D. et al., Beijing — unverified here).
- No source retrieved gave a definitive global age-standardized incidence trend with confidence intervals adjusted for diagnostic intensity.

## Key Question 4: Environmental risk factors — pesticides, TCE/solvents, air pollution, head trauma, well water, metals, rural living, Camp Lejeune

### Takeaway
Pesticides (paraquat and rotenone OR ~2.5 in FAME; any pesticide pooled OR ~1.5–1.6), TCE (Camp Lejeune HR ~1.7; ambient TCE RR 1.10 top vs bottom decile in 1.3 M Medicare beneficiaries), fine particulate air pollution (~10% increased risk per 5 µg/m³ PM2.5), and traumatic brain injury (HR 1.5–1.8) are the best-supported environmental risks; rural living, well water, farming and lead carry modest pooled ORs (~1.2–1.4), while manganese and dietary metals show little or no association.

### Cited Findings

**Pesticides**
- FAME (Farming and Movement Evaluation) case-control study nested in the Agricultural Health Study (110 PD cases, 358 controls): paraquat OR 2.5 (95% CI 1.4–4.7), rotenone OR 2.5 (1.3–4.7); mitochondrial complex-I-inhibiting pesticides as a group OR 1.7 (1.0–2.8); oxidative-stress-inducing pesticides OR 2.0 (1.2–3.6) — [Tanner et al., Rotenone, Paraquat, and Parkinson's Disease, Environmental Health Perspectives 2011](https://ehp.niehs.nih.gov/doi/full/10.1289/ehp.1002839); [EHP news summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3114841/).
- Meta-analysis of 105 articles (random effects): any pesticide use RR 1.56 (1.37–1.77; 49 studies); herbicides 1.20 (1.00–1.43); insecticides 1.46 (1.01–2.11); fungicides 0.96 (0.74–1.25); paraquat 1.47 (1.01–2.13; 13 studies); farming 1.24 (1.12–1.37; 48 studies); rural living 1.43 (1.22–1.69; 29 studies); well water 1.30 (1.12–1.51; 35 studies). Note: after publication-bias correction, pesticide associations became non-significant in random-effects models (the authors were industry-affiliated, which the report writer may wish to flag) — [Breckenridge et al., Association between PD and Cigarette Smoking, Rural Living, Well-Water Consumption, Farming and Pesticide Use: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, PLoS One 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4824443/).
- Pesticide/herbicide/fungicide exposure summarized at OR ~1.4–1.8 in a 2024 review (citing Ritz, Ascherio, Agricultural Health Study) — [Grotewold & Albin, Update: Protective and risk factors for Parkinson disease, Parkinsonism & Related Disorders 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).
- Other key pesticide/solvent studies: [Pezzoli & Cereda, Exposure to pesticides or solvents and risk of Parkinson disease, Neurology 2013 (meta-analysis)](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e318294b3c8); [Pesticide use and incident PD in a cohort of farmers and their spouses (Agricultural Health Study), 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344245886_Pesticide_use_and_incident_Parkinson's_disease_in_a_cohort_of_farmers_and_their_spouses); [Firestone et al., Pesticides and risk of PD: a population-based case-control study, Archives of Neurology 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15642854); [Hancock et al., Pesticide exposure and risk of PD: a family-based case-control study, BMC Neurology 2008](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2323015/); causal-inference discussion in [Pesticides and Parkinson's disease: causal relationship at the population and individual level?, Journal of Neural Transmission 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00702-025-03054-3); screening approaches in [Towards improved screening of toxins for Parkinson's risk, npj Parkinson's Disease 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-023-00615-9).
- Noyce 2012 meta-analysis also found significant positive associations for pesticide exposure, head injury, rural living, farming occupation, well-water drinking, and beta-blocker use — [Noyce et al., Annals of Neurology 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687).

**Trichloroethylene (TCE) and solvents; Camp Lejeune**
- Cohort of ~340,000 service members: PD risk was 70% higher in Marines stationed at Camp Lejeune, NC, during 1975–1985 (water contaminated with TCE and other volatile organic compounds) than at Camp Pendleton — [Goldman SM et al., Risk of Parkinson Disease Among Service Members at Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune, JAMA Neurology 2023;80(7):673–681](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2805037); [accompanying editorial, The Water, the Air, the Marines—Camp Lejeune, Trichloroethylene, and Parkinson Disease, JAMA Neurology 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37184845/); [CNN coverage](https://www.cnn.com/2023/05/15/health/camp-lejeune-parkinsons-tce).
- Population-based case-control of US Medicare beneficiaries ≥67 y (221,789 incident PD cases, 1,132,765 matched controls, 2016–2018) linked to residential ambient TCE in 2002: dose-dependent association, RR 1.10 for top vs lowest decile of ambient TCE, adjusted for age, sex, race, smoking, healthcare use, rurality and PM2.5 — [Krzyzanowski B et al., Ambient Trichloroethylene Exposure and Parkinson Disease Risk in Medicare Beneficiaries, Neurology 2025](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214174); [editorial, Trichloroethylene and Parkinson Disease, Neurology 2025](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214245).
- Organic solvents (particularly TCE) summarized at OR ~1.4 — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).

**Air pollution**
- Updated meta-analysis of 42 studies: OR 1.10 (95% CI 1.03–1.17) for PD per 5 µg/m³ long-term PM2.5 — [Ambient Air Pollution and PD and AD: An Updated Meta-Analysis, Toxics 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxics13020139).
- Systematic review/meta-analysis (19 North American, 12 European, 10 Asian studies): PD risk +10% per 5 µg/m³ PM2.5 (1.10; 1.03–1.19) and +18% per PM10 increment (1.18; 1.01–1.38), largest PM2.5 estimate in Asia (1.19; 1.01–1.41); no association of PM2.5/NO2 with MS — [Association of long-term outdoor air pollution exposure with incidence of PD, MS and motor neuron diseases, Environment International 2026 (medRxiv 2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412026003351).
- Air pollution summarized at OR 1.1–1.2 — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).

**Traumatic brain injury**
- Veterans Health Administration cohort: all-severity TBI HR 1.71 (1.53–1.92); mild TBI HR 1.56 (1.35–1.80); moderate-severe TBI HR 1.83 (1.61–2.07) — [Gardner RC et al., Mild TBI and risk of Parkinson disease: A Chronic Effects of Neurotrauma Consortium Study, Neurology 2018](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000005522).
- Among trauma patients ≥55 y, TBI vs non-brain trauma: PD 1.7% vs 1.1%, adjusted HR 1.44 (1.31–1.58) over 5–7 years — [Gardner et al., Traumatic brain injury in later life increases risk for Parkinson disease, Annals of Neurology 2015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ana.24396); meta-analysis: [TBI and risk of PD: a meta-analysis, Acta Neurologica Belgica 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13760-023-02209-x). TBI history did not alter progression in two early-PD cohorts — [2025 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11926254).

**Metals**
- Systematic review/meta-analysis: cumulative bone lead associated with PD (ORs 1.34 [1.02–1.76] and 1.32 [1.04–1.66], dose-response); occupational manganese pooled OR 1.04 (1.01–1.06; 4 studies); occupational lead OR 1.14 (0.64–2.01); general metal exposure OR 1.22 (0.70–2.14); dietary iron OR 0.99, dietary manganese OR 1.11 (0.70–1.76); serum copper, iron and zinc lower in PD; authors conclude evidence is "limited" — [Metal Exposure and Risk of Parkinson Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, American Journal of Epidemiology 2023](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/192/7/1207/7109767).
- Lead summarized at OR ~1.5–1.6 (Gunnarsson & Bodin meta-analysis) — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).

**Well water, rural living**
- Pooled ORs above from Breckenridge 2016 (well water 1.30; rural 1.43; farming 1.24). A separate meta-analysis of 15 case-control studies found no significant overall association for well water — [Well-Water Consumption and Risk of Parkinson's Disease: A Meta-Analysis, Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment](https://www.dovepress.com/well-water-consumption-and-risk-of-parkinsons-disease-a-meta-analysis--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NDT). Rural California well-water consumption linked to pesticide-contaminated water — [Gatto et al., Well-Water Consumption and PD in Rural California, Environmental Health Perspectives 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2799466/).

**Dairy, diabetes, infections (risk)**
- Dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohorts: high dairy RR 1.26 (1.12–1.41), low-fat dairy 1.30 (1.06–1.58), milk 1.23; 5–7% higher risk per 100 g/day dairy — [Foods and Dietary Intakes and the Risk of PD: Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41252180/).
- Type 2 diabetes: observational OR 1.21 (1.07–1.36); faster motor progression; MR supports causal effect (IVW OR 1.08, 1.02–1.14) — [Chohan et al., Type 2 Diabetes as a Determinant of PD Risk and Progression, Movement Disorders 2021](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.28551); multivariable MR: [Diabetology & Metabolic Syndrome 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13098-024-01299-8).
- H. pylori OR ~1.5–1.9 (eradication did not reduce risk); hepatitis C OR ~1.2–1.5 (treatment reduces risk); COVID-19 evidence conflicting — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).

### Inferences
- Effect sizes for environmental exposures are individually modest (RR 1.1–1.8) except for specific high-dose exposures (paraquat/rotenone, Camp Lejeune); the population impact argument rests on ubiquity of exposure (TCE, PM2.5) rather than per-person risk.
- The Krzyzanowski 2025 ambient-TCE finding is the first large population-level dose-response demonstration beyond occupational/military settings and strengthens the case that TCE is a general-population risk.
- The Breckenridge meta-analysis' attenuation of pesticide effects after publication-bias correction is an outlier conclusion in the literature and should be weighed against its authors' industry affiliation (noted in the paper's disclosures; not verified here).

### Gaps
- Paraquat-specific prospective (non-case-control) cohort estimates and the ongoing US EPA regulatory status of paraquat were not researched.
- No primary data were retrieved on perchloroethylene (PCE) specifically.
- Exact hazard ratio and confidence interval from Goldman 2023 (press summaries say "70% higher") were not extracted from the paper.

## Key Question 5: Protective/inverse associations and causal inference — caffeine, smoking, urate, physical activity, NSAIDs, statins, GLP-1 agonists; Mendelian randomization

### Takeaway
Smoking (current vs never RR ~0.41–0.44) and caffeine (OR ~0.7) remain the most robust inverse associations, with MR broadly supporting smoking's protective effect but mixed for coffee; physical activity (RR 0.77) is consistent and dose-dependent; urate's protective association failed both MR and the SURE-PD3 inosine trial; NSAIDs, statins and calcium channel blockers show weak/inconsistent observational protection with negative trials; GLP-1 agonists produced a positive phase 2 (lixisenatide, NEJM 2024) but a negative phase 3 (exenatide, Lancet 2025).

### Cited Findings

**Smoking**
- Current vs never smoking RR 0.44 (0.39–0.50) — [Noyce et al., Annals of Neurology 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687); current smoking RR 0.41 (0.34–0.48; 33 studies), heavy/long-term smoking 0.49 (0.43–0.57; 37 studies) — [Breckenridge et al., PLoS One 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4824443/).
- OR ~0.4–0.6; MR supports neuroprotective effect though reverse causation remains plausible; the NIC-PD nicotine trial was negative — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).
- MR of smoking and drinking behaviours: ever-smoking reduces PD risk — [Genetic evidence for protective effects of smoking and drinking behavior on PD: MR study, medRxiv 2020](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.20.20073247.full.pdf); [Domenighetti et al., Unhealthy Behaviours and Risk of Parkinson's Disease: A Mendelian Randomisation Study, Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34275906/).
- Mechanistic review of smoking and coffee components — [Smoking, coffee intake, and Parkinson's disease: Potential protective mechanisms and components, Neurotoxicology 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0161813X24001530).

**Caffeine / coffee**
- Coffee drinking showed a significant negative association in the Noyce 2012 meta-analysis — [Noyce et al. 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687); OR ~0.7–0.75, mechanism proposed via adenosine A2A receptor blockade, possible reverse causation — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).
- MR of plasma caffeine levels and AD/PD — [Plasma Caffeine Levels and Risk of AD and PD: MR Study, 2022](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9102212). A 2025 MR found coffee consumption associated with later PD age-at-onset but not with risk or progression; prior MR studies failed to find a causal effect on risk — [Coffee consumption is associated with later age-at-onset of Parkinson's disease, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11844581/).

**Urate**
- OR ~0.6–0.9 observationally; SURE-PD3 inosine trial negative; MR "no causal relationship" — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/); [Kia et al., MR Study Shows No Causal Relationship Between Circulating Urate Levels and PD, Annals of Neurology 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481555/). Earlier MR suggested a causal protective effect of urate on PD progression specifically — [Simon et al., Mendelian randomization of serum urate and PD progression, Annals of Neurology 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.24281). Low plasma urate in men was added as a prodromal marker in the 2019 MDS criteria — [Heinzel et al. 2019](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.27802).

**Physical activity**
- Meta-analysis of 21 observational studies (13 cohort, 8 case-control): RR 0.77 (0.70–0.85); 9% lower risk per 10 MET-h/week — [Physical activity and risk of PD: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis, Journal of Neurology 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-024-12672-y); earlier: [Fang et al., Association of Levels of Physical Activity With Risk of PD, JAMA Network Open 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2703134); women (E3N cohort): [Association of Physical Activity and PD in Women, Neurology 2023](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000207424); accelerometer patterns: [npj Digital Medicine 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01135-3); NIH-AARP: [Xu et al., Physical activities and future risk of PD, Neurology 2010](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e3181ea1597).
- OR ~0.65 for moderate-to-strenuous activity; light exercise shows no association — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).

**NSAIDs, statins, calcium channel blockers, antihypertensives**
- NSAIDs OR ~0.8–0.9, ibuprofen ~0.6–0.7; calcium channel blockers OR ~0.7–0.9 with negative STEADY-PD (isradipine) trial; statins OR ~0.7–1.2 with negative phase II simvastatin trial — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).
- Regular ibuprofen use associated with ~one-third lower PD risk in HPFS/NHS cohorts — [Gao et al., Use of ibuprofen and risk of Parkinson disease, Neurology 2011](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e31820f2d79); a Norwegian population-based study found no decreased incidence with NSAIDs — [Parkinson's News Today summary](https://parkinsonsnewstoday.com/news/nsaids-no-effect-parkinsons-disease-incidence-study/).
- Noyce 2012 found negative associations for NSAIDs, calcium channel blockers, hypertension and alcohol, and a positive association for beta-blockers — [Noyce et al. 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687).
- Statins (β = 5.6 years) and NSAIDs (β = 4.1 years) were among the strongest independent predictors of later PD age at onset in a 2025 cohort analysis — [Adrenergic blockers, statins, and NSAIDs are associated with later age at onset in PD, Journal of Neurology 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-025-12989-2).

**GLP-1 receptor agonists**
- Phase 2 lixisenatide trial (early PD): motor scores largely stable on drug vs progression on placebo at 12 months; nausea 46%, vomiting 13% — [Meissner et al., Trial of Lixisenatide in Early Parkinson's Disease, NEJM 2024](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2312323); [MJFF summary](https://www.michaeljfox.org/news/results-parkinsons-trial-diabetes-drug-lixisenatide-published).
- Phase 3 Exenatide-PD3 (UK, six centers; largest and longest GLP-1 RA trial in PD): no evidence exenatide slowed progression — [Vijiaratnam et al., Lancet, February 2025 — Cure Parkinson's summary](https://cureparkinsons.org.uk/2025/02/exenatide-pd3-results-published/); [MJFF summary](https://www.michaeljfox.org/news/exenatide-glp-1-drug-shows-no-impact-parkinsons-symptoms); [Parkinson's Foundation](https://www.parkinson.org/blog/science-news/ozempic-treatment).

**Mendelian randomization summary**
- MR supports causal roles for smoking (protective) and type 2 diabetes (risk), finds no causal effect of urate, and is mixed/negative for coffee on risk; IBD/TNF polymorphisms show no association — [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/); [Chohan et al. 2021](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.28551); [Kia et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481555/); [Domenighetti et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34275906/).

### Inferences
- The pattern "strong observational protection, negative MR and negative trial" (urate, isradipine, simvastatin, nicotine) suggests that much observational protection reflects reverse causation from prodromal behavioural change (loss of smell/taste reducing coffee and cigarette use; reduced activity) — consistent with the Lancet 2024 seminar's warning about the long prodromal period.
- Smoking is the one exposure where MR agrees with observation, yet the NIC-PD trial failed, implying either that the protective agent is not nicotine or that prevention-era effects cannot be recapitulated after diagnosis.
- GLP-1 RA evidence is now genuinely conflicting; differences in compound CNS penetration (lixisenatide vs exenatide) are the leading hypothesis but untested.

### Gaps
- Exact point estimates/CIs from the lixisenatide (MDS-UPDRS III difference) and exenatide trials were not extracted from the primary papers.
- MR evidence for physical activity and for NSAID/statin use was not located.
- Alcohol: only the Noyce 2012 inverse association and the Domenighetti MR mention were retrieved; no updated 2024–2026 synthesis.

## Key Question 6: Prodromal risk factors (RBD, hyposmia, constipation, depression) and the MDS prodromal criteria

### Takeaway
Isolated RBD is the strongest prodromal marker (6.3%/year phenoconversion, 73.5% at 12 years); hyposmia (OR 5.2 in lowest quartile, predictive ~4 years ahead), constipation (RR 2.34) and depression/anxiety are established, and the 2019 MDS research criteria combine these with risk markers (now including diabetes, cognitive deficit, inactivity, low urate in men) in a Bayesian likelihood framework validated in population, RBD and LRRK2 cohorts.

### Cited Findings
- Multicentre iRBD cohort (n≈1,280): overall phenoconversion to a neurodegenerative syndrome 6.3% per year, 73.5% after 12 years; mean follow-up 4.6 years (range 1–19); conversion risk increased with abnormal quantitative motor testing, objective motor signs, olfactory deficit, MCI, and others — [Postuma et al., Risk and predictors of dementia and parkinsonism in idiopathic REM sleep behaviour disorder: a multicentre study, Brain 2019;142(3):744–759](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/142/3/744/5353011); meta-analysis of longitudinal RBD studies — [Sleep Medicine Reviews 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1087079218300819); trial-design implications — [Neuroprotective Trials in REM Sleep Behavior Disorder, Neurology 2022](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000200235).
- Honolulu-Asia Aging Study (2,267 men aged 71–95, 8 y follow-up, 35 incident PD): lowest odor-identification quartile vs top two quartiles OR 5.2 (95% CI 1.5–25.6); association significant only within the first 4 years, so impaired olfaction "can predate clinical PD in men by at least 4 years" — [Ross GW, Petrovitch H, Abbott RD, Tanner CM et al., Association of olfactory dysfunction with risk for future Parkinson's disease, Annals of Neurology 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.21291).
- Constipation RR 2.34 (1.55–3.53); history of anxiety or depression significantly associated with later PD — [Noyce et al., Annals of Neurology 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ana.23687).
- Meta-analyses of non-motor symptom prevalence before and after diagnosis — [Chen et al., Translational Neurodegeneration 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2047-9158-4-1).
- MDS research criteria (2019 update): naive Bayesian classifier combining age with risk and prodromal markers to estimate probability of prodromal PD; validated in general-population, RBD and LRRK2 cohorts with high specificity/positive predictive value but variable sensitivity depending on depth of marker assessment; new markers added: diabetes, global cognitive deficit, physical inactivity, low plasma urate (men); web-based calculator released — [Heinzel S, Berg D, Gasser T, Chen H, Yao C, Postuma RB et al., Update of the MDS research criteria for prodromal Parkinson's disease, Movement Disorders 2019](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.27802).
- Retrospective general-population validation of the MDS criteria — [A retrospective study of the MDS criteria for prodromal PD in the general population, npj Parkinson's Disease 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-024-00739-6); overview — [Prodromal Parkinson's disease, Revue Neurologique 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0035378725005739).
- GBA1 carriers show increased depression, RBD and hyposmia — [Vieira et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11657020/); GBA1 rs3115534 associates with RBD in Nigerians — [GP2](https://gp2.org/genetic-representation-in-parkinsons-research-drives-progress/).

### Inferences
- The prodromal literature is the key to interpreting protective associations: markers like hyposmia and depression plausibly change coffee/smoking/exercise behaviour years before diagnosis.
- RBD cohorts are now the preferred population for prevention trials because of their high and quantifiable conversion rate.

### Gaps
- No specific hazard ratio for depression as a prodromal risk factor (e.g., Gustafsson 2015 Neurology) was retrieved; only the Noyce 2012 meta-analytic direction is cited.
- Sensitivity/specificity numbers for the MDS criteria in specific cohorts were not extracted.

## Key Question 7: Major cohort studies and consortia (PPMI, Rotterdam, HPFS/NHS, UK Biobank, Fox Insight, GP2, Agricultural Health Study, Honolulu-Asia, Rochester)

### Takeaway
PD epidemiology and genetics rest on a small set of infrastructures: PPMI (MJFF, since 2010; >1,500 participants including de novo PD, controls, LRRK2/GBA/SNCA carriers and prodromal RBD/hyposmia groups), GP2 (>100,000 samples genotyped, 343,000 planned), Fox Insight (~56,000 online participants), UK Biobank (source of 18,618 proxy cases in Nalls 2019 and GBA1 penetrance data), and the classic population cohorts (Rotterdam, HPFS/NHS, Honolulu-Asia, Agricultural Health Study, Rochester Epidemiology Project).

### Cited Findings
- PPMI: launched by MJFF in 2010 as an observational, international, longitudinal study to establish biomarker-defined cohorts and identify clinical, imaging, genetic and biospecimen progression markers; >1,500 participants at 33 sites; initial cohort completed April 2013 with 423 de novo PD, 196 controls, 64 SWEDD; plus 65 prodromal (hyposmia/RBD), 372 LRRK2, 246 GBA, 27 SNCA carriers (affected and unaffected); collects motor/non-motor data, DaT imaging, blood/urine/CSF — [Marek et al., The Parkinson's progression markers initiative (PPMI) – establishing a PD biomarker cohort, Annals of Clinical and Translational Neurology 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acn3.644); [The Parkinson Progression Marker Initiative, Progress in Neurobiology 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21930184/); [MJFF PPMI page](https://www.michaeljfox.org/news/parkinsons-progression-markers-initiative-ppmi).
- Fox Insight (MJFF online study): 56,063 participants (site count, Oct 2026); people with PD and loved ones complete online questionnaires every 90 days plus one-time topic surveys; de-identified data shared with qualified researchers — [Fox Insight](https://foxinsight.michaeljfox.org/).
- GP2: see Q2 — 436 cohorts, 84 locations, 103,786 samples analyzed, 343,123 expected — [Flores-Ocampo et al., Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13435180/).
- UK Biobank: supplied 18,618 proxy cases (first-degree relative with PD) to Nalls 2019 — [Nalls et al., Lancet Neurology 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1474442219303205); GBA1 pathogenic variants present in 8.8% of UK Biobank PD cases — [2025 GBA1 penetrance study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40506446/).
- Rotterdam Study: population-based cohort providing benchmark PD incidence — [de Lau et al., Neurology 2004](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/01.WNL.0000140706.52798.BE).
- Health Professionals Follow-up Study / Nurses' Health Study: source of ibuprofen, caffeine, dairy and urate findings (e.g., [Gao et al., Neurology 2011](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0b013e31820f2d79)); Ascherio group work summarized in [Grotewold & Albin 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11846500/).
- Agricultural Health Study / FAME: [Tanner et al., EHP 2011](https://ehp.niehs.nih.gov/doi/full/10.1289/ehp.1002839); [AHS cohort 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344245886_Pesticide_use_and_incident_Parkinson's_disease_in_a_cohort_of_farmers_and_their_spouses).
- Honolulu-Asia Aging Study: [Ross et al., Annals of Neurology 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.21291).
- Rochester Epidemiology Project: [Savica et al., JAMA Neurology 2016](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2529538); [Savica et al., PD with and without dementia: prevalence and projections, Movement Disorders 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5892791/).
- Parkinson's Foundation Prevalence/Incidence Projects: [Marras 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326232975_Prevalence_of_Parkinson's_disease_across_North_America); [Willis 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-022-00410-y).
- Medicare-based environmental epidemiology (Krzyzanowski/Racette group, Washington University) — [Neurology 2025](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214174).
- Camp Lejeune/Camp Pendleton veterans cohort — [Goldman et al., JAMA Neurology 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2805037).

### Inferences
- MJFF (via PPMI, Fox Insight, and implementation of ASAP-funded GP2) is the dominant private funder shaping both the genetic and prodromal/biomarker landscape; the field's 2024–2026 outputs (Kim 2024, RAB32, GBA1 rs3115534, lixisenatide) trace back to these infrastructures.
- Administrative-claims datasets (Medicare, VA) have become the workhorse for environmental exposure epidemiology at national scale.

### Gaps
- Current UK Biobank incident PD case counts and PPMI's current (2026) enrollment totals and prodromal-cohort expansion figures were not retrieved.
- Fox Insight launch year and 23andMe genetic sub-study details were not confirmed from the site.
