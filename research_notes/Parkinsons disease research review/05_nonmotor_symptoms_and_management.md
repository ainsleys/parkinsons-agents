# Non-Motor Symptoms of Parkinson's Disease and Their Management (literature through 2026)

## Key Question 1: Cognitive impairment and dementia — prevalence, time course, PD-MCI criteria, PDD vs DLB, biomarkers, treatment

### Takeaway
Dementia was long held to be near-inevitable in long-surviving PD (83% at 20 years in the Sydney cohort), but 2024 data from two large modern prospective cohorts put the 10-year dementia probability at only 9–27%, suggesting dementia is less frequent or later than older studies implied. PD-MCI (MDS Task Force criteria, Litvan 2012) affects roughly a quarter to half of early PD and is a harbinger of dementia; GBA1 and APOE-ε4 carriage and low CSF amyloid-β predict faster decline. Rivastigmine remains the only cholinesterase inhibitor with a positive large RCT (EXPRESS, Emre 2004 NEJM); donepezil's phase 3 missed its primary endpoint, memantine helped DLB but not PDD, and nothing has worked for PD-MCI (rivastigmine, rasagiline) or disease-modification of PDD (ambroxol, 2025). The one-year rule separating PDD from DLB is under active challenge from biological (α-synuclein-seed-based) definitions proposed in 2024, but a 2025 Nature Reviews Neurology perspective argues a single α-synuclein assay cannot replace clinicopathological distinctions.

### Cited Findings

**Prevalence and time course**
- Sydney Multicenter Study (Hely MA et al., Movement Disorders 2008;23:837–844): one group of clinicians followed a single cohort of newly diagnosed PD for 20 years; dementia was present in 83% of 20-year survivors (25 of 30); dementia correlated with increasing age and "probably reflects an interplay of multiple pathologies"; eight had diffuse Lewy bodies as the only cause of dementia, others had mixed pathology — [Hely 2008, Mov Disord](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.21956); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227800791_The_Sydney_Multicenter_Study_of_Parkinson%27s_disease_the_inevitability_of_dementia_at_20_years)
- Companion neuropathology paper on longitudinally followed Sydney patients: Halliday G et al., "The progression of pathology in longitudinally followed patients with Parkinson's disease," Acta Neuropathologica 2008 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00401-008-0344-8)
- Retrospective on the Sydney study published 2026: Hely M, "The Sydney Multicenter Study of Parkinson's Disease: The Why, the How, and the Outcome," Movement Disorders 2026 May;41(5):1096–1100, doi 10.1002/mds.70245 — [PubMed 41841437](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41841437/). Search-result summaries of this paper state the cohort was 136 newly diagnosed patients enrolled from 1984, 74% had died by 20 years, and 83% of survivors were demented; a standardized mortality ratio of 3.1 for years 15–20 was also quoted in a search summary but could not be verified against the abstract (see Gaps).
- Gallagher J, Gochanour C, Caspell-Garcia C, ... Weintraub D; PPMI, "Long-Term Dementia Risk in Parkinson Disease," Neurology 2024 Sep 10;103(5):e209699: motivated by the observation that "it is widely cited that dementia occurs in up to 80% of patients with PD, but studies reporting such high rates were published over two decades ago, had relatively small samples." PPMI cohort (n=417 de novo PD, mean age 61.6): estimated dementia probability at 10 years of disease duration was 9% (site-investigator diagnosis), 15% (MoCA <21) or 12% (MDS-UPDRS Part I cognition ≥3). Penn cohort (n=389, mean age 69.3): 47% eventually diagnosed with dementia; median time to dementia 15 years (95% CI 13–15); probability 27% at 10 years, 50% at 15 years, 74% at 20 years. Conclusion: "dementia in PD occurs less frequently, or later in the disease course, than previous research studies have reported" — [PubMed 39110916](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39110916/)
- Multicentre retrospective of dementia in long-term PD: "Dementia in long-term Parkinson's disease patients: a multicentre retrospective study," npj Parkinson's Disease 2019 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-019-0106-4)
- Major reviews: Aarsland D et al., "Cognitive decline in Parkinson disease," Nature Reviews Neurology 2017;13 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrneurol.2017.27); Aarsland D et al., "Parkinson disease-associated cognitive impairment," Nature Reviews Disease Primers 2021;7 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41572-021-00280-3); "Characteristics and mechanisms of cognitive impairment in Parkinson disease," Nature Reviews Neurology 2025 — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41582-025-01163-x)

**PD-MCI criteria and prevalence**
- Litvan I, Goldman JG, Tröster AI, Schmand BA, Weintraub D, Petersen RC, Mollenhauer B, Adler CH, Marder K, Williams-Gray CH, Aarsland D, Kulisevsky J, Rodriguez-Oroz MC, Burn DJ, Barker RA, Emre M, "Diagnostic criteria for mild cognitive impairment in Parkinson's disease: Movement Disorder Society Task Force guidelines," Movement Disorders 2012;27(3):349–356. Criteria are based on literature review and expert consensus; MCI "is common in nondemented PD patients and may be a harbinger of dementia"; a two-level scheme (Level I abbreviated assessment; Level II comprehensive neuropsychological testing with at least two tests in each of five cognitive domains) — [PubMed 22275317](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22275317/)
- In a large early-PD cohort, between 22.8% and 53.7% fulfilled PD-MCI criteria depending on the operationalisation; in incident PD the frequency was 42.5% using Level II criteria at 1.5 SD below norms — [Frontiers in Cognition 2024 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cognition/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2024.1369538/full); [Yarnall et al., Neurology 2014, "Characterizing mild cognitive impairment in incident Parkinson disease" (ICICLE-PD)](https://www.neurology.org/doi/abs/10.1212/wnl.0000000000000066)

**Biomarkers of cognitive decline (GBA, APOE, amyloid)**
- 10-year population-based study: carriers of APOE-ε4 and GBA variants had faster cognitive decline and higher risk of progression to dementia; risk was further increased in carriers of both genotypes — ["GBA and APOE Impact Cognitive Decline in Parkinson's Disease: A 10-Year Population-Based Study" (PMC9362732)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9362732/)
- Longitudinal multi-cohort analysis of cognitive decline pattern in GBA1-PD (2025 preprint) — [medRxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.01.25341351.full.pdf)
- In a large cohort of newly diagnosed PD, PD-MCI correlated significantly with lower CSF β-amyloid 1–42 and 1–40 — [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00712/full)
- Comorbid Alzheimer pathology profoundly shapes clinicopathological heterogeneity of Lewy body diseases — ["Clinicopathological Heterogeneity of Lewy Body Diseases: The Profound Influence of Comorbid Alzheimer's Disease," medRxiv 2024](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.30.24312864.full.pdf); review of Lewy-related pathology, α-synuclein oligomers and comorbid pathologies in Lewy body dementia, Molecular Neurodegeneration 2025 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13024-025-00900-6)

**PDD vs DLB, the one-year rule, and 2024–2025 reclassification efforts**
- One-year rule: DLB is diagnosed when dementia precedes or begins within one year of parkinsonism; PDD when dementia begins more than one year after parkinsonism. The fourth DLB Consortium consensus (McKeith IG et al., Neurology 2017) "remains supportive of the 1-year rule distinguishing DLB from PD dementia, because as originally stated this arbitrary cutoff remains useful, particularly in clinical practice" — [McKeith 2017, Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000004058)
- Earlier argument against merging the entities ("Arguing against the proposed definition changes of PD") — [PMC5168716](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5168716/)
- Biological definitions proposed in 2024: Höglinger GU, Adler CH, Berg D, Klein C, Outeiro TF, Poewe W, Postuma R, Stoessl AJ, Lang AE, "A biological classification of Parkinson's disease: the SynNeurGe research diagnostic criteria," Lancet Neurology 2024;23(2):191–204 — three-component system: pathological α-synuclein (S) in tissue/CSF, neurodegeneration (N) on imaging, pathogenic gene variants (G), linked to a clinical component (C) — [PubMed 38267191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38267191/). Simuni T, Chahine LM, Poston K, ... Marek K, "A biological definition of neuronal α-synuclein disease: towards an integrated staging system for research," Lancet Neurology 2024;23(2):178–190 (NSD-ISS) — [PubMed 38267190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38267190/). Both frameworks would, in effect, subsume PD, PDD and DLB under one biologically defined entity — [Alzforum summary](https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/biological-definition-parkinsons-disease-two-proposals-pave-way); [MDS Scientific Issues commentary](https://www.movementdisorders.org/MDS/Scientific-Issues/posts/Exploring-the-path-towards-a-biological-definition-of-PD-and-its-widespread-implications.htm)
- Pushback: "Concerns with the new biological research criteria for synucleinopathy," Lancet Neurology 2024 — [Lancet Neurology](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(24)00214-X/fulltext); Höglinger GU & Lang AE, "SynNeurGe: The road ahead for a biological definition of Parkinson's disease," Journal of Parkinson's Disease 2025 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1877718X241298194)
- Fu Y & Halliday GM, "Dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson disease dementia — the same or different and is it important?" Nature Reviews Neurology 2025 Jul;21(7):394–403: the availability of α-synuclein assays "has sparked new interest in a single disease diagnosis for all individuals positive for α-synuclein"; the authors argue "an α-synuclein assay alone is unlikely to be a specific criterion for a spectrum of clinical syndromes with Lewy pathology or a definitive diagnostic marker for Lewy body dementia," that "one biological assay will not reflect the complex spatiotemporal features of brain pathology," and that "diverse sequential mechanisms underpin the highly heterogeneous phenotypes" — [PubMed 40355531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40355531/)
- NSD-ISS performance in PPMI, PASADENA and SPARK baseline cohorts — [npj Parkinson's Disease 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-024-00789-w)

**Treatment: positive, equivocal and failed trials**
- EXPRESS trial: Emre M, Aarsland D, Albanese A, Byrne EJ, Deuschl G, De Deyn PP, Durif F, Kulisevsky J, van Laar T, Lees A, Poewe W, et al., "Rivastigmine for dementia associated with Parkinson's disease," NEJM 2004;351(24):2509–2518. 541 patients with mild–moderate dementia developing ≥2 years after PD diagnosis, rivastigmine 3–12 mg/day vs placebo for 24 weeks; ADAS-cog improved 2.1 points (baseline 23.8) vs 0.7-point worsening on placebo (P<0.001); clinically meaningful ADCS-CGIC improvement in 19.8% vs 14.5%, worsening in 13.0% vs 23.1% (P=0.007); more nausea, vomiting and tremor with rivastigmine; effects "moderate and similar to those reported in trials of rivastigmine for Alzheimer's disease" — [PubMed 15590953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15590953/)
- Dubois B, Tolosa E, Katzenschlager R, Emre M, Lees AJ, et al., "Donepezil in Parkinson's disease dementia: a randomized, double-blind efficacy and safety study," Movement Disorders 2012;27(10):1230–1238. n=550, donepezil 5 or 10 mg vs placebo, 24 weeks; ADAS-cog not significant by the predefined model (−1.45, P=.050 for 5 mg; −1.45, P=.076 for 10 mg); an alternative analysis removing the treatment-by-country term showed dose-dependent benefit (−2.08, P=.002; −3.31, P<.001); CIBIC+ significant only at 10 mg (P=.040); MMSE, executive function and attention improved; "the study did not achieve its predefined primary end points" — [PubMed 22915447](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22915447/)
- Emre M, Tsolaki M, Bonuccelli U, Destée A, Tolosa E, et al., "Memantine for patients with Parkinson's disease dementia or dementia with Lewy bodies: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial," Lancet Neurology 2010;9(10):969–977. n=199; at 24 weeks ADCS-CGIC improved with memantine in DLB (3.3 vs 3.9, p=0.023) but not in PDD (3.6 vs 3.8, p=0.576) or the total population (p=0.120); NPI improved in DLB only (−5.9, p=0.041) — [PubMed 20729148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20729148/)
- Mamikonyan E, Xie SX, Melvin E, Weintraub D, "Rivastigmine for mild cognitive impairment in Parkinson disease: a placebo-controlled study," Movement Disorders 2015;30(7):912–918. n=28 crossover with transdermal rivastigmine; primary ADCS-CGIC showed only a trend (P=0.096); no effect on any cognitive measure — [PubMed 25914281](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25914281/)
- MODERATO: Weintraub D, Hauser RA, Elm JJ, Pagan F, Davis MD, Choudhry A, "Rasagiline for mild cognitive impairment in Parkinson's disease: a placebo-controlled trial," Movement Disorders 2016;31(5):709–714. n=170, 24 weeks; SCOPA-Cog change not different (LS mean difference 0.8, 95% CI −0.48 to 2.05, P=0.22); no MoCA or CGIC difference — [PubMed 27030249](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27030249/)
- ReSPonD: rivastigmine for gait stability in PD, phase 2, Lancet Neurology 2016 (cholinergic approach to falls) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474442215003890)
- Ambroxol: Silveira CRA, Coleman KKL, Borron K, ... Pasternak SH, "Ambroxol as a Treatment for Parkinson Disease Dementia: A Randomized Clinical Trial," JAMA Neurology 2025;82(8):797–807. 52-week phase 2, single centre, 55 randomized (ambroxol 525 or 1050 mg/day vs placebo); more GI adverse events with ambroxol (12% vs 5% of AEs); CSF ambroxol 0.73 μM; β-glucocerebrosidase levels higher at week 26 (12.45 vs 8.50 nmol/h/mg, P=.05); "no evidence to suggest differences between groups on primary or secondary outcomes" (ADAS-Cog-13, CGIC) — target engagement shown but cognitive effect "not confirmed" — [PubMed 40587145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40587145/)
- MDS EBM review (Seppi 2019, see Key Question 7): rivastigmine "efficacious / clinically useful" for PDD; donepezil and galantamine "insufficient evidence / possibly useful"; memantine "insufficient evidence / investigational"; for non-dementia cognitive impairment, rivastigmine, rasagiline, tDCS and cognitive rehabilitation all "insufficient evidence / investigational" — "no clinically useful interventions identified to treat non-dementia-level cognitive impairment" — [Seppi et al., Mov Disord 2019 (PMC6916382)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6916382/)
- Pimavanserin in PDD psychosis (HARMONY subgroup) — [Parkinsonism & Related Disorders 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1353802023010301)

### Inferences
- The gap between Sydney (83% at 20 y) and PPMI/Penn (9–27% at 10 y; 74% at 20 y in Penn) is partly reconciled by age at onset and follow-up length: the Penn 20-year estimate (74%) approaches Sydney's, while the younger de novo PPMI cohort has not yet reached long durations. The "80% inevitable" framing is being replaced by "roughly half by 15 years, most by 20 years in older-onset clinic cohorts."
- The treatment landscape has not materially changed since 2004: a single cholinesterase inhibitor with modest effect, no approved therapy for PD-MCI, and the first GBA-targeted trial (ambroxol) negative on cognition despite target engagement.
- Reclassification is a live 2024–2026 debate: biological staging frameworks (SynNeurGe, NSD-ISS) would dissolve the PDD/DLB boundary, while neuropathologists (Fu & Halliday 2025) and the DLB Consortium argue the one-year rule still has clinical and research utility because co-pathology and spatiotemporal spread, not α-synuclein positivity alone, drive phenotype.

### Gaps
- The abstract of Hely 2026 (Mov Disord 41:1096) could not be retrieved (publisher 403; PubMed record lacks abstract). Numbers attributed to it above (136 patients, 74% dead at 20 y, SMR 3.1) come from search-engine summaries and should be verified.
- Full-text arguments of Fu & Halliday 2025 on the one-year rule beyond the abstract were not accessible.
- No specific 2024–2025 "Lewy body disease" renaming consensus document beyond SynNeurGe/NSD-ISS was located within the search budget.
- Galantamine RCT data and the Cochrane review of cholinesterase inhibitors for PDD/DLB were not retrieved.

## Key Question 2: Psychosis — prevalence, hallucinations, pimavanserin, clozapine, quetiapine

### Takeaway
Hallucinations affect roughly 40% of treated PD when minor phenomena are counted (Fénelon 2000), are driven more by cognitive impairment, sleep-wake disturbance and disease duration than by dopaminergic drugs alone, and are the single strongest predictor of nursing-home placement. Low-dose clozapine (PSG, NEJM 1999) and pimavanserin (Cummings, Lancet 2014; FDA approval 2016) are the two agents rated efficacious by the MDS; quetiapine's RCTs were negative; and pimavanserin's real-world mortality signal remains contested, with one Medicare long-term-care cohort showing increased 1-year mortality (HR 1.56) and an FDA Medicare new-user study showing 35% lower mortality than other atypicals in the first 180 days but no advantage in nursing homes.

### Cited Findings
- Fénelon G, Mahieux F, Huon R, Ziégler M, "Hallucinations in Parkinson's disease: prevalence, phenomenology and risk factors," Brain 2000;123:733–745. 216 consecutive patients: hallucinations in the prior 3 months in 39.8%; minor forms (presence, passage, illusions) 25.5%, formed visual hallucinations 22.2%, auditory 9.7%; independent predictors of formed visual hallucinations were severe cognitive disorder, daytime somnolence and long disease duration; "a simple side-effect of dopaminergic treatment is not sufficient to explain the occurrence of all visual hallucinations" — [PubMed 10734005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10734005/)
- Clinic-population prevalence of hallucinations ranges 8–44% — [Systematic review of quetiapine in parkinsonism, J Neuropsychiatry Clin Neurosci](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.neuropsych.18080180)
- Hallucinations and institutionalization: Aarsland D, Larsen JP, Tandberg E, Laake K, J Am Geriatr Soc 2000;48:938–942 — in 178 community-dwelling PD patients followed 4 years, 26.4% were admitted to nursing homes; old age, functional impairment, dementia and hallucinations were independent predictors and "the presence of hallucinations was the strongest predictor" — [PubMed 10968298](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10968298/). Earlier: Goetz CG & Stebbins GT, "Risk factors for nursing home placement in advanced Parkinson's disease," Neurology 1993 — [PubMed 8232934](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8232934/); Goetz & Stebbins, "Mortality and hallucinations in nursing home patients with advanced PD," Neurology 1995 — [PubMed 7723953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7723953/)
- Clozapine: Parkinson Study Group, "Low-dose clozapine for the treatment of drug-induced psychosis in Parkinson's disease," NEJM 1999;340(10):757–763. 60 patients, 6.25–50 mg/day (mean 24.7 mg) for 4 weeks; CGI improved 1.6 vs 0.5 (P<0.001), BPRS 9.3 vs 2.6 (P=0.002), SAPS 11.8 vs 3.8 (P=0.01); tremor improved; no worsening of parkinsonism; one discontinuation for leukopenia — [PubMed 10072410](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10072410/)
- Clozapine is "the only antipsychotic with proven efficacy in PD psychosis available outside the United States but apprehensions about its adverse event profile result in substantial underuse" — [Systematic review, J Neuropsychiatry Clin Neurosci](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.neuropsych.18080180). Rater-blinded quetiapine vs clozapine comparison (Merims et al. 2006): clozapine had a significant advantage in reducing delusions and a non-significant trend for hallucinations — [PubMed 17095896](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=17095896). Long-term single-centre retrospective of clozapine for quetiapine-refractory PD psychosis (Pirker et al., Parkinson's Disease 2025) — [PMC11991777](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11991777/)
- Quetiapine: of four randomized double-blind placebo-controlled quetiapine trials, three found no significant improvement in psychosis on any measure; one small study showed improvement in sleep and visual hallucinations — [Shotbolt et al., RCT of quetiapine, Neuropsychiatr Dis Treat 2009 (PMC2699657)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2699657/); [Systematic review of double-blind RCTs of antipsychotics in PDP (PMC12984574)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12984574/)
- Pimavanserin pivotal trial: Cummings J, Isaacson S, Mills R, Williams H, Chi-Burris K, Corbett A, Dhall R, Ballard C, "Pimavanserin for patients with Parkinson's disease psychosis: a randomised, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial," Lancet 2014;383(9916):533–540 (study ACP-103-020). Selective 5-HT2A inverse agonist, 40 mg/day, 6 weeks, after a 2-week non-pharmacological lead-in to limit placebo response; 199 randomized, 185 analysed; SAPS-PD change −5.79 vs −2.73 (difference −3.06, 95% CI −4.91 to −1.20; p=0.001; Cohen's d 0.50); 10 vs 2 discontinuations for adverse events; no worsening of motor function; funded by ACADIA — [PubMed 24183563](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24183563/)
- FDA approval (2016) and pre-marketing mortality signal: across the development program, deaths were 5.3% on pimavanserin vs 0.5% on placebo and the FDA's reviewing psychiatrist recommended against approval; the drug was nonetheless approved in 2016 for hallucinations and delusions of PD psychosis — [Journal of Integrative Neuroscience 2024 review](https://www.imrpress.com/journal/JIN/23/4/10.31083/j.jin2304080); [Neurology: Clinical Practice pimavanserin overview](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/CPJ.0000000000000342). Mosholder et al. note that "in premarketing trials in patients with PD psychosis, 11% of patients died during open-label pimavanserin treatment" — [PubMed 35702829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35702829/)
- Mortality controversy, cohort 1: Hwang YJ, Alexander GC, An H, Moore TJ, Mehta HB, "Risk of Hospitalization and Death Associated With Pimavanserin Use in Older Adults With Parkinson Disease," Neurology 2021;97(13):e1266–e1275. Medicare-certified long-term-care residents ≥65 (2,186 users vs 18,212 nonusers, IPTW-weighted): 30-day hospitalization aHR 1.24 (1.06–1.43); 90-day mortality aHR 1.20 (1.02–1.41), 180-day 1.28 (1.13–1.45), 1-year 1.56 (1.42–1.72). Class II evidence — [PubMed 34389652](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34389652/)
- Mortality controversy, cohort 2 (FDA): Mosholder AD, Ma Y, Akhtar S, ... Graham DJ, "Mortality Among Parkinson's Disease Patients Treated With Pimavanserin or Atypical Antipsychotics: An Observational Study in Medicare Beneficiaries," American Journal of Psychiatry 2022;179(8):553–561. New-user cohort, pimavanserin (N=3,227) vs atypical antipsychotics (N=18,442), 2016–2019; mortality ~35% lower with pimavanserin in the first 180 days (HR 0.65, 0.53–0.79), no advantage beyond 180 days (HR 1.05) and "no mortality advantage in nursing home patients" — [PubMed 35702829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35702829/)
- Narrative review (Rissardo et al., Brain Sciences 2022): hallucinations are "a more frequent cause of institutionalization than motor disability or dementia"; meta-analytic efficacy of pimavanserin is inferior to clozapine but with fewer side effects — [PMC9599742](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9599742/)
- MDS EBM (Seppi 2019): clozapine and pimavanserin "efficacious / clinically useful"; quetiapine "insufficient evidence / possibly useful"; olanzapine "non-efficacious / not useful" — [PMC6916382](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6916382/)
- Ongoing head-to-head safety/sedation trial of pimavanserin vs low-dose quetiapine — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04164758 protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/58/NCT04164758/Prot_000.pdf)

### Inferences
- The two Medicare analyses reach opposite conclusions largely because of comparators and setting: Hwang compared users with non-users in nursing homes (confounding by indication likely), whereas Mosholder compared against other antipsychotics (an active-comparator design) and still found no benefit in nursing-home residents. The consistent message is that pimavanserin offers no clear mortality advantage in the frailest, institutionalized population.
- Quetiapine's widespread use rests on tolerability and the impracticality of clozapine monitoring, not on RCT efficacy.

### Gaps
- Exact FDA approval date (April 2016) and the Nuplazid boxed-warning language were not confirmed from a primary FDA document within the budget.
- The French Clozapine Parkinson Study Group Lancet 1999 trial was not retrieved.

## Key Question 3: Depression, anxiety, apathy and impulse-control disorders

### Takeaway
Clinically significant depressive symptoms affect ~35% of PD (major depression ~17%), anxiety disorders ~31%, and apathy ~40%; half of apathetic patients are neither depressed nor cognitively impaired, supporting apathy as a separate syndrome. Dopamine agonists raise ICD odds 2.7-fold cross-sectionally (DOMINION, 13.6% point prevalence) and carry a ~46% five-year cumulative incidence with a clear dose–duration effect (Corvol 2018); ICDs regress after agonist withdrawal. For depression, paroxetine and venlafaxine XR (SAD-PD) and pramipexole (Barone 2010) have positive RCTs and CBT has large effect sizes; the MDS rates pramipexole and venlafaxine "clinically useful." No RCT met MDS inclusion criteria for anxiety, and naltrexone for ICDs was negative on its primary endpoint.

### Cited Findings

**Prevalence**
- Reijnders JS, Ehrt U, Weber WE, Aarsland D, Leentjens AF, "A systematic review of prevalence studies of depression in Parkinson's disease," Movement Disorders 2008;23(2):183–189. 36 articles: weighted prevalence of major depressive disorder 17%, minor depression 22%, dysthymia 13%; clinically significant depressive symptoms 35%; MDD 19% with structured interview vs 7% without; "the average prevalence of major depressive disorder in PD is substantial, but lower than generally assumed" — [PubMed 17987654](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17987654/)
- Broen MP, Narayen NE, Kuijf ML, Dissanayaka NN, Leentjens AF, "Prevalence of anxiety in Parkinson's disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis," Movement Disorders 2016;31(8):1125–1133. 45 articles: average point prevalence of anxiety disorders 31%; GAD 14%, social phobia 13.8%, anxiety NOS 13.3%, specific phobia 13.0%, panic 6.8%; clinically significant anxiety symptoms by cutoff 25.7% — [PubMed 27125963](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27125963/)
- den Brok MG, van Dalen JW, van Gool WA, Moll van Charante EP, de Bie RM, Richard E, "Apathy in Parkinson's disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis," Movement Disorders 2015;30(6):759–769. 23 studies, n=5,388: apathy prevalence 39.8% (95% CI 34.6–45.0); associated with higher age (+3.3 y), lower MMSE (−1.4), comorbid depression RR 2.3, higher UPDRS motor (+6.5); 57.2% of apathetic patients had concomitant depression; "half of patients with apathy do not suffer from concomitant depression or cognitive impairment, confirming its status as a separate clinical syndrome" — [PubMed 25787145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25787145/)

- The SAD-PD finding that paroxetine's effect exceeded venlafaxine's numerically (6.2 vs 4.2 HAM-D points) while the MDS rated venlafaxine but not SSRIs "efficacious" reflects the MDS requirement for ≥2 positive high-quality trials per drug and the mixed earlier SSRI trial record, not evidence that SSRIs are inferior.
- The DOMINION cross-sectional OR of 2.7 understates lifetime risk: in agonist-treated incident patients followed prospectively, roughly half develop an ICD within 5 years.

### Gaps
- Anxiety-specific RCTs (e.g., buspirone pilot, CBT for anxiety) and any post-2019 positive anxiety trial were not retrieved.
- The original Dobkin 2011 AJP RCT abstract was not retrieved directly (cited via the pilot paper and a 2018 systematic review).
- No 2022–2026 meta-analysis of ICD prevalence was located within the search budget.

## Key Question 4: Autonomic dysfunction — orthostatic hypotension, GI, urinary, sexual, sialorrhea, sweating

### Takeaway
Orthostatic hypotension affects about one third of PD (pooled 33% in a 2026 meta-analysis of 55 studies/10,463 patients; neurogenic OH 26%), typically appearing a decade into disease, whereas OH within the first year favours MSA. Droxidopa (Kaufmann, Neurology 2014; FDA 2014) is the only agent with phase 3 RCT support and is MDS-rated "efficacious" short-term; midodrine and fludrocortisone rest on insufficient evidence. For constipation, probiotics/prebiotic fibre are rated "efficacious," macrogol and lubiprostone "likely efficacious"; botulinum toxin A/B is "efficacious / clinically useful" for sialorrhea (meta-analytic SMD −0.98 on drooling scores); sildenafil is "efficacious" for erectile dysfunction; solifenacin has insufficient evidence for overactive bladder; and no RCTs exist for sweating.

### Cited Findings

**Orthostatic hypotension**

## Key Question 5: Sleep — RBD, insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness, restless legs, glymphatic hypothesis

### Takeaway
RBD affects ~42% of PD (pooled, 28 studies) and rises with age and disease duration; isolated RBD converts to a synucleinopathy at ~6% per year (73.5% by 12 years; Postuma, Brain 2019), making it the strongest prodromal marker. First-line clonazepam and melatonin rest mainly on open-label experience: the only placebo-controlled RCTs in PD-RBD (clonazepam, Shin 2019; prolonged-release melatonin, Gilat 2020) were both negative, and the MDS found no RBD RCT meeting criteria. EDS affects ~35% (pooled); modafinil has only "possibly useful" status and sodium oxybate improved sleepiness in a 12-patient crossover trial but induced sleep apnoea. The glymphatic hypothesis gained human support in 2025: a reduced DTI-ALPS index in iRBD predicted phenoconversion to PD in a multi-cohort MRI study.

### Inferences

### Gaps
- Pooled insomnia and RLS prevalence meta-analyses specific to PD were not retrieved.
- Nelotanserin, ramelteon and other RBD trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov were seen only as registry entries, not results.

## Key Question 6: Pain and sensory symptoms — pain classification, hyposmia, visual dysfunction, fatigue

### Takeaway
Pain is reported by anywhere from 20% to 98% of PD patients depending on definition (commonly "up to 85%"), and since 2015 can be graded with the King's PD Pain Scale (Chaudhuri, Movement Disorders 2015; 7 domains, 14 items, 0–168), MDS-recommended and now validated in many languages. Hyposmia affects 75–90% and precedes motor onset by years; self-reported visual complaints are common (unclear vision 21.7%, difficulty reading 21.6%); fatigue affects roughly 58% (PRIAMO) and up to two-thirds. Treatment evidence is weak: rasagiline is MDS-rated "efficacious / possibly useful" for fatigue, oxycodone-naloxone PR has insufficient evidence for pain, and no RCTs exist for olfactory or ophthalmologic dysfunction.

- The 20–98% spread in pain prevalence is a definitional artefact; the adoption of KPPS and its cut-offs should narrow future estimates and allow phenotype-specific trials (e.g., fluctuation-related pain responding to dopaminergic optimisation vs central pain).
- Sensory domains (olfaction, vision) are far better characterised as prodromal/diagnostic markers than as treatment targets; no symptomatic therapy exists for either.

### Gaps
- No RCT of a pain intervention in PD since the oxycodone-naloxone PR trial (PANDA, 2015) was retrieved; the search budget was exhausted before pain-trial queries.
- Quantitative prevalence data for colour-vision and contrast-sensitivity deficits from primary studies were not retrieved.

## Key Question 7: Assessment tools (NMSQuest, NMSS, MDS-NMS, MDS-NMS-Q) and the MDS evidence-based review of non-motor treatments

### Takeaway
The King's/MDS lineage of instruments progressed from the 30-item self-completed NMSQuest screener (Chaudhuri, Movement Disorders 2006) to the rater-administered 30-item, 9-domain NMSS (2007), the 13-domain MDS-NMS with a non-motor-fluctuations subscale (2020), and the self-completed 52-symptom MDS-NMS-Q (Weintraub, Movement Disorders 2025). The MDS Evidence-Based Medicine Committee's Seppi 2019 review (37 new studies, literature to 31 Dec 2016) remains the most recent comprehensive MDS non-motor treatment review found; it rated only a handful of interventions "efficacious / clinically useful" (pramipexole and venlafaxine for depression; rivastigmine for dementia; clozapine and pimavanserin for psychosis; BoNT-A/B for drooling; probiotics/prebiotic fibre for constipation; sildenafil for ED) and found no RCTs for anxiety, RBD, sweating, olfaction or vision.

## Key Question 8: Non-motor burden — how many NMS does a typical patient carry, and how do they drive quality of life and institutionalization?

### Takeaway
Virtually every PD patient has non-motor symptoms (98.6% in PRIAMO, 99.7% on MDS-NMS), carrying on average ~8 (PRIAMO/NMSS), ~10 (NMSQuest) or ~16 (MDS-NMS) symptoms, with the count rising with disease duration (7.5 to 9.5 over 3 years in early PD). Martinez-Martin's NMSS validation cohort (n=411) showed NMSS total correlates more strongly with PDQ-39 (rS=0.70) than motor severity does (rS=0.58) and is the best single predictor of health-related quality of life, with depression, anxiety, constipation/incomplete emptying, concentration/memory complaints and insomnia the most damaging items. Hallucinations and dementia, together with age and functional impairment, independently predict nursing-home placement, and hallucinations are the strongest single predictor (Aarsland 2000).

### Inferences
- The clinically useful heuristic for 2026 is "about ten non-motor symptoms per patient, nearly all patients affected, and the ones that matter most for quality of life are mood, cognition, sleep and bowel function, not tremor."
- Because hallucinations and dementia are the dominant drivers of institutionalization, and because the only treatments rated "clinically useful" for them (clozapine, pimavanserin, rivastigmine) have modest effect sizes or safety trade-offs, the non-motor burden remains the principal unmet need in long-term PD care.

### Gaps
- A Martinez-Martin-authored study specifically modelling institutionalization risk from NMSS scores was not located; the institutionalization evidence above comes from Aarsland (2000) and Goetz (1993/1995).
- Caregiver-burden quantification tied to NMS counts was not retrieved.
- The PRIAMO primary paper abstract (Barone 2009) was accessed only via publisher landing page/secondary summaries; the 98.6% and 7.8 figures are as reported by those sources.
